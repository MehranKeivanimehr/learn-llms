## Not letters, not words: tokens

In level 1 we pretended the model predicts *words*. Real LLMs predict **tokens** — chunks of text. A common word like "the" is usually one token. A rare word gets split into several pieces, like "token" + "izer".

Why not just use whole words? There are too many of them (and new ones appear every day). Why not single letters? Then every sentence becomes very long, and the model has to do much more work. Tokens are the compromise.

## How a tokenizer learns its pieces

Most LLMs use **byte-pair encoding (BPE)**. It starts with single characters and repeats one simple step:

1. Find the pair of neighbouring tokens that appears most often.
2. Merge that pair into a new, longer token everywhere it appears.

After thousands of merges, common words and word-parts become single tokens. Try it — spaces are shown as ␣:

## Why LLMs struggle to count letters

Because the model sees tokens, not letters, a question like "how many r's are in *strawberry*?" is surprisingly hard. The model might see something like this:

The letters are hidden inside the tokens. The model has to have *learned* how each token is spelled — it can't just look.
