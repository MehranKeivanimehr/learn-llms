## One trick, repeated

A large language model (LLM) — the technology behind AI chatbots — does one thing: it looks at the text so far — the **context** — and guesses what comes next. Then it adds that guess to the context and guesses again. A long answer is just this step, repeated many times.

The guess isn't a single word. It's a **probability for every word the model knows**. After "The capital of France is", "Paris" might get 90%, "a" 2%, and thousands of other words share the rest.

## What's going on inside?

This toy model keeps a score for how strongly each word *pulls* toward each possible next word: "France" pulls hard toward "Paris", "Germany" toward "Berlin". Recent words pull harder than older ones. The model adds up all the pulls and turns the totals into probabilities.

Real LLMs don't use a hand-written table. They *learn* billions of numbers from huge amounts of text, and they weigh every word in a long context in subtle ways — that's what the next levels are about. But the output is the same kind of thing: a probability for every possible next token.
